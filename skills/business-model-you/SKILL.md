# Business Model You — Personal Career Canvas

Apply the Business Model Canvas to a person. The purpose is not to reduce someone to a business; it is to make visible the structure that usually stays implicit — who actually values your work, what they truly pay for, what it costs you to deliver, and whether that arrangement is sustainable.

The most common failure of career advice is treating a job title as an identity. This skill treats a job as *one current configuration* of a model that can be redesigned.

| Situation | Route |
|---|---|
| A company, product, startup, or business idea | Use `business-model-canvas` instead |
| A person's trajectory, skills, satisfaction, positioning | This skill |
| **Solopreneur / freelancer / consultant** — a one-person business | This skill, with a nested business canvas for the practice itself. The person and the practice have *different* models: the practice can be profitable while the person is burning out. Analyze both and name where they diverge. |
| Job search or application positioning | This skill, emphasizing Value Proposition and Customer Segments |

## Step 0: Gather context — but infer generously

Work from what the person has already shared before asking anything. People find it tedious to be interviewed about themselves, and much of the canvas can be inferred from a CV, a description of their work, or prior conversation.

Ask at most 2–3 questions, and only for blocks that are genuinely unrecoverable and load-bearing. The highest-value questions are usually:
- What parts of the work energize you, and what parts drain you? (Cost Structure + real Revenue Streams)
- Who has actually paid for or sought out your work, and for what specifically? (Customer Segments + Value Proposition)
- What decision is this analysis feeding? (Determines depth and emphasis)

If analyzing someone other than the user, use only public professional information, stay respectful, and treat it as a positioning lens rather than a judgment of the person.

**When the subject IS the reader** (someone asking about their own career), adjust register deliberately:
- **Be more direct, not less.** The reader can verify every claim against their own experience and will detect hedging or flattery instantly — both destroy the analysis's usefulness.
- **Do not soften the weakest block.** The reason they asked is to find it. Softening it wastes the exercise.
- **Do not inflate the strongest block either.** Overstating what they already know to be true costs credibility for the findings they don't.
- **Separate observation from inference explicitly.** "Your homepage leads with X" is an observation; "which under-sells the combination" is an inference they may reject. Keeping the seam visible lets them disagree with the conclusion without discarding the evidence.
- **Assume competence.** They know their field better than you do. The value added is structural — seeing the shape of the model — not domain expertise.

**Calibrate to career stage** — the same canvas means different things:

| Stage | Where the analysis should focus |
|---|---|
| **Early (0–5 yrs)** | Key Resources are still being built; the question is *which* resources to invest in. Value Proposition is often borrowed from the employer's. Don't over-diagnose a thin canvas as a weak one. |
| **Mid (5–15 yrs)** | The canvas is real and testable. Focus on coherence: do resources, activities, and revenue actually align, or has drift set in? This is where the strongest insight usually lands. |
| **Senior / late** | Key Resources are substantial; the questions become leverage (how does the value scale beyond your own hours?), succession, and legacy/meaning as genuine revenue. |
| **Pivot / transition** | Explicitly map *two* canvases — current and target — and analyze the gap: which resources transfer, which must be built, which segments must be re-earned. |

**Sector calibration.** Some fields have structurally different canvases, and applying the default reading to them produces confident wrong answers:

- **Academia and research institutions.** Tenure is simultaneously a Key Resource (security, credibility), a Cost (the non-portable asset that makes leaving expensive), and an Option (the freedom to take decade-horizon risk) — analyze it in all three blocks rather than picking one. "Customers" include grant panels and tenure committees, which decide but do not benefit. Value capture is *designed* to be low in exchange for autonomy and problem-selection freedom; treat that as a deliberate trade, not a defect. Students and open artifacts are the primary multiplier channel, and their value accrues to other institutions by design.
- **Public sector, nonprofit, medicine, clinical, and mission-driven work.** Same principle: the model deliberately trades capture for mission, stability, or service. Name the trade-off; do not diagnose it as underpricing.
- **Regulated professions** (law, medicine, accounting): credentials function as a moat that constrains as well as protects — switching segments often means re-earning licensure, which belongs in Cost Structure as a real barrier.

## Step 1: The nine blocks, personal edition

Work in this order. It moves from the outside in — who you serve, then what it takes — which is the opposite of how people usually think about careers, and that inversion is where the insight comes from.

### 1. Customer Segments — who benefits from your work
Not just "my employer." Distinguish:
- **Who pays** (employer, clients, funders)
- **Who decides** (hiring manager, review committee, procurement)
- **Who benefits** (end users, colleagues, patients, students, readers)
These are often three different parties, and confusing them is a common source of career frustration — doing excellent work for the beneficiary while being evaluated by the decider.
Note concentration: one employer = single-customer dependency.

### 2. Value Propositions — the outcomes you deliver
Never a job title. Express as: *what problem do you solve, for whom, that they couldn't easily solve otherwise?*
Classify the type: newness, performance, customization, "getting the job done," risk reduction, accessibility, convenience, status. Then apply the harder test:
- **What would break if you left?** (the honest measure of value delivered)
- **What can you do that few others in your market can?** (the distinctiveness test)
- Is your value proposition *legible* — can the people who decide actually see it?

### 3. Channels — how people find and evaluate you
Cover all five phases: awareness → evaluation → purchase (hiring/contracting) → delivery → after-sales (reputation, referrals). Typical channels: professional network and referrals, published work, portfolio, conference talks, LinkedIn/social, recruiters, open-source or public artifacts, institutional affiliation.
The diagnostic question: **can opportunities reach you without your active effort?** If every opportunity requires you to push, the channel block is weak regardless of how strong the value is.

### 4. Customer Relationships — how trust is built and kept
Personal/1:1 (mentorship, close collaboration), dedicated (a key client or manager relationship), community (professional networks, field reputation), automated/self-serve (published work that speaks for you while you sleep).
Diagnostic: is your reputation *portable* (attached to you, travels with you) or *institutional* (attached to your employer's brand, evaporates on exit)?

### 5. Revenue Streams — everything you actually get back
Two categories, both real:
- **Hard**: salary, bonus, equity, fees, royalties, benefits, pension, security
- **Soft**: learning, autonomy, meaning, recognition, relationships, flexibility, optionality for the future

Soft returns are not a consolation prize — they are the reason many people stay in roles that underpay, and ignoring them makes the analysis wrong.

**Two distinct cases; do not conflate them.**
- **Deliberate trade** — the person knowingly chose lower hard revenue *for* autonomy, mission, flexibility, or field-building, and is getting what they bargained for. This is a valid design. Name the trade-off and its costs, but do not frame it as being underpaid or exploited. Academia, public service, and mission-driven work are usually this case.
- **Undeliberate subsidy** — soft returns are offered *in place of* hard ones the person actually wants, or once-real soft returns (learning, autonomy) have quietly eroded while hard compensation never rose to compensate. This is worth naming plainly.

The distinguishing question: **would they make the same trade again today, knowing what they now know?** If yes, it's a design. If the soft returns have thinned while the costs stayed, it's a subsidy. Ask rather than assume.
Diagnostic: are you capturing value proportional to what you create? A large gap between value created and value captured is the single most common finding.

### 6. Key Resources — what you have
- **Skills and knowledge**: technical, domain, tacit craft
- **Experience**: what you've actually done, especially the rare or hard-won
- **Personality and disposition**: how you work, not just what you know
- **Network**: who knows and vouches for you
- **Credentials and reputation**: degrees, publications, track record, public artifacts
- **Financial**: runway, which determines how much risk you can take

Assess each: which are **scarce** (few have them), which are **appreciating** (worth more over time), which are **depreciating** (obsolescing)? A resource base heavy in depreciating assets is a slow-motion risk even when current income looks fine.

### 7. Key Activities — what you actually do
Distinguish sharply:
- Activities that **produce your core value** (the ones customers would pay for directly)
- Activities that **enable** them (necessary maintenance)
- Activities that are **neither** (organizational overhead, meetings that produce nothing)

Estimate rough time allocation. The gap between where time goes and where value comes from is usually the most actionable finding in the entire canvas.

### 8. Key Partnerships — who amplifies you
Mentors and sponsors (distinguish these: a mentor advises you, a **sponsor spends their own capital advocating for you** — the second is far scarcer and far more consequential), collaborators, teams, institutions whose brand transfers to you, professional communities.
Diagnostic: do you have sponsors, or only mentors? Do your partnerships create obligations that constrain you?

### 9. Cost Structure — what it takes out of you
- **Time**: hours, and their distribution (are they contiguous enough for deep work?)
- **Energy**: which activities drain vs. restore — this is not the same as time
- **Money**: training, tools, commuting, relocation, forgone income
- **Stress and health**: the cost most often omitted and most often decisive
- **Opportunity cost**: what this path forecloses — the most invisible cost of all

Diagnostic: is the model **cost-driven** (optimizing for stability and efficiency) or **value-driven** (accepting high cost for high impact/growth)? Neither is wrong, but mismatch with the person's actual stage and obligations is.

**Evidence discipline:** mark claims as [stated] (the person said it), [inferred] (reasonable deduction, flagged as such), or [unknown] (a gap worth naming). Do not fill blocks with plausible-sounding invention — an honest blank is more useful, because the gap itself is often the finding.

## Step 2: Identify the career archetype

Name the closest pattern; most people are a blend, and blends are worth naming as blends:

| Archetype | Shape | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Specialist** | Deep expertise, narrow segment | High value per unit, defensible | Obsolescence; narrow market |
| **Generalist / connector** | Bridges domains or functions | Adaptable, high option value | Value can be illegible; "jack of all trades" perception |
| **Platform / multiplier** | Value scales beyond own hours (teaching, tooling, leading, publishing) | Leverage; income decoupled from time | Requires building an asset before it pays |
| **Portfolio** | Multiple parallel streams | Diversified, autonomous | Fragmented attention; high channel overhead |
| **Institutional insider** | Value tied to one organization's context | Deep trust, real internal power | Non-portable; single-customer dependency |
| **Public-artifact / audience** | Reputation built on visible published output | Opportunities flow inbound | Requires sustained production; visibility ≠ income |

Also ask: is the person's *actual* archetype the one they *think* they are? A common and costly mismatch is someone with specialist resources operating in an institutional-insider configuration, accumulating trust that doesn't travel.

## Step 3: Coherence and honest critique

This is where the analysis earns its keep. Be direct — flattery here is a disservice, since the whole point is to surface what's not working.

**Fit checks:**
- Do your **Key Activities** actually produce your **Value Proposition**, or has your time drifted toward activities that produce something else?
- Do your **Channels** reach the **Segments** that most value what you do — or are you visible mainly to people who value you least?
- Do your **Revenue Streams** (hard + soft) reflect the value you create, or are you subsidizing someone?
- Does your **Cost Structure** match the returns? Value-driven costs (long hours, high stress) paired with cost-driven returns (flat salary, no equity, no learning) is the definition of an unsustainable model.

**Risk checks:**
- **Concentration**: single employer, single skill, single network, single sponsor
- **Depreciation**: are core resources obsolescing faster than you're rebuilding them?
- **Portability**: how much of your value evaporates if you change organizations?
- **Sustainability**: is the cost structure survivable for years, or only for a sprint? Name burnout risk plainly when the pattern is there.
- **Legibility**: can decision-makers actually perceive your value, or does it require excavation?

**Deliver a verdict**: name the 2–3 strongest blocks and the 2–3 weakest, with reasoning. Then state the single highest-leverage change — the one adjustment that would improve the most blocks at once.

## Step 4: What's next — the recommended-moves plan

The canvas is diagnosis; this is the prescription, and it is what the person actually came for. A list of possible improvements is not a plan — a plan is sequenced, costed, and states what to do *first*.

### 4a. Draw the moves from canvas logic

Each move must name the block it changes and the trade-off it carries. Standard move types:

- **Add or shift a segment** — serve a market that values your existing resources more highly
- **Sharpen the value proposition** — same work, legible framing; specificity beats breadth
- **Open a channel** — publish, speak, or build a public artifact so opportunities arrive inbound
- **Convert soft revenue to hard** — turn recognition or learning into title, compensation, or equity
- **Invest in an appreciating resource** — naming explicitly which depreciating one you're letting go
- **Cut a cost** — drop an activity consuming energy without producing value; note its political cost
- **Activate a partnership** — specifically a sponsor, not another mentor
- **Add leverage** — move from doing the work to multiplying it (tools, teaching, team, publication)

### 4b. Score each move

| Move | Block it fixes | Effort | Time to payoff | Reversible? | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Reversibility is the most under-weighted column.** Cheap, reversible moves should be run early and in parallel — they generate information at low cost. Expensive irreversible moves (leaving a role, relocating, closing a business) should be made last, after the reversible ones have produced evidence.

### 4c. Sequence by horizon

- **Now (0–30 days)** — cheap, reversible, information-generating. Usually: activate a dormant relationship, reframe how the work is described, publish something already written. These should be startable the day the analysis is read.
- **Near (1–6 months)** — build or open something: a channel, a segment, a skill. Requires sustained effort but not irreversible commitment.
- **Structural (6–18 months)** — the moves that change the model's shape: role change, pivot, leverage shift, renegotiation. Should depend on evidence produced by the earlier horizons.

Respect dependencies explicitly. Build the resource before opening the channel that advertises it; gather evidence before making the irreversible commitment.

### 4d. Name the first move

State **one** move as the place to start, and why it dominates: highest leverage per unit of effort, unblocks the most other moves, or generates the information the harder decisions need. If someone does only one thing from the analysis, this is it.

### 4e. State what NOT to do

As valuable as the recommendations, and usually omitted. Cover:
- Moves that look attractive but address a block that isn't the constraint (e.g. more credentials when the problem is legibility, not qualification)
- Strengths not to trade away in pursuit of a fix
- Anything the analysis specifically does not support, so the plan isn't over-read

### 4f. Decision triggers

Career plans are made under uncertainty. Name 2–3 **observable** events that would change the recommendation — an offer arriving, a resource depreciating faster than expected, a soft-revenue stream thinning, a market shift. This converts a static plan into one that survives contact with reality.

## Output format

**Citations in files must be real links.** Never write `<cite index="...">` citation markup into a file. That markup only resolves inside the chat interface, where it is matched against search results in context; written to a document it becomes inert text with nothing to resolve against, and it survives conversion to .docx or .pdf as visible junk. In files, cite with ordinary markdown links — `[the claim text](https://url)` — and close the document with a numbered **Sources** list mapping each externally-sourced claim to its URL. Chat responses use citation markup normally; files never do.


1. **One-line model summary** — e.g., "A specialist model with strong resources, weak channels, and institutional rather than portable reputation"
2. **The canvas** — nine blocks, 2–4 bullets each, evidence-labeled
3. **Archetype** (and any mismatch with self-perception)
4. **Coherence check** — strengths, weaknesses, risks, and the single highest-leverage change
5. **What's next** — scored move table, three horizons (now / near / structural), the single first move, what not to do, and decision triggers

Keep it dense. For a transition question, present current and target canvases side by side and analyze the gap explicitly.

Default to markdown in chat. Create a file if the analysis is long or the person wants to keep it.

## Guardrails

- **This is a strategic lens, not therapy or life advice.** Career dissatisfaction sometimes has causes the canvas cannot see. If someone is describing genuine distress, burnout, or crisis, respond to the person first; the framework is secondary and can wait.
- **Don't flatter.** A canvas that reports everything is strong is useless. But critique the *model*, not the person's worth — the entire premise is that a model is a configuration that can be changed.
- **Don't pathologize a stage.** An early-career canvas is legitimately thin. Say so rather than diagnosing weakness.
- **Respect what the person values.** If someone has deliberately chosen lower pay for meaning or flexibility, that's a valid design — note the trade-off, don't override the preference.
- **Never invent facts about the person.** Unknown blocks stay unknown and get named as questions worth answering.
- **For third parties**, keep to public professional information and avoid speculation about private circumstances.
