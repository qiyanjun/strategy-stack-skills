# Personal Power Analysis — 7 Powers for a Career

Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers* defines Power as the conditions producing **persistent differential returns**. Two requirements, and both must hold:

- **Benefit** — the advantage materially improves outcomes (compensation, opportunity, autonomy, influence).
- **Barrier** — competitors cannot or will not replicate it, even knowing it works.

**The Barrier is what career advice systematically omits.** "I'm a strong Python programmer," "I'm good with clients," "I work hard" are benefits with no barrier — anyone can acquire them, and increasingly a model can approximate them. They describe *competence*, which is table stakes for entry, not Power, which determines who captures value.

This skill does one thing: separate the person's real Powers from their commodity strengths, and identify which Powers are buildable from where they stand.

---

## Step 0: Define the arena and the foils

**Power is not absolute. It is always relative to a specific market and specific rivals.** Analysis that skips this produces flattery.

Establish, asking only if it cannot be inferred:

1. **The arena** — what specific role, market, or opportunity is this defensibility *for*? "Senior ML engineer at a frontier lab" and "independent AI consultant to mid-market firms" demand almost disjoint power packages. If the person names no arena, ask; this one question determines the entire analysis.
2. **The foils** — who else credibly competes for that same thing? Name 2–3 realistic profiles ("PhD researchers with publication records but no production experience," "staff engineers from big tech without research background"). Every power rating below is *versus these people*, never versus an abstraction.
3. **The current outcome gap** — is the person getting the opportunities they want? If yes, powers are working and the question is durability. If no, the diagnosis is usually one of three things: no real power, real power that is illegible, or real power aimed at the wrong arena. Step 4 distinguishes these.

## Step 1: The seven powers, personal edition

For each power, run both tests. A power counts only if **both** pass. Rate: **absent / latent / real / strong**.

### 1. Scale Economies → Leverage economics

*Business version: unit costs fall as volume rises.*
**Personal version:** the cost of delivering your value does not rise linearly with the number of people served.

- **Benefit test:** can your work reach ten times more people without ten times the hours? Assets that do this: published artifacts, open-source tools, courses, books, frameworks others apply, teams you've built, systems you've designed.
- **Barrier test:** is the underlying asset costly to rebuild? A blog post is not a barrier. A library the field depends on, a curriculum others adopt, or a trained team is.
- **Absent when:** income is strictly hourly — consulting, billable work, individual contribution with no artifact. Perfectly linear effort-to-value is the definition of no scale economy.
- **How to build:** convert repeated work into an artifact — the second time you explain something, write it down; the third time, tool it.

### 2. Network Economies → Compounding network value

*Business version: the product improves as more users join.*
**Personal version:** your value to each person in your network increases as the network grows.

- **Benefit test:** do people seek you out *because* of who else you know or convene? Do introductions flow through you? Does your recommendation carry weight because of the aggregate?
- **Barrier test:** could a well-resourced newcomer assemble the same network quickly? Networks built on trust and time resist this; networks built on job title do not — they belong to the title.
- **Common holders:** community builders, conference organizers, prolific advisors whose alumni now reinforce each other, hub connectors between fields.
- **How to build:** convene rather than attend. The organizer accrues network economies; the participant does not.

### 3. Counter-Positioning → The move peers can't copy

*Business version: a new model incumbents won't adopt because it damages their existing business.*
**Personal version:** a way of working that established peers *could* copy but *won't*, because it would devalue what they've already invested in.

This is the most underused power in career strategy and the most available to people without seniority.

- **Benefit test:** are you doing something that works and that respected peers visibly avoid?
- **Barrier test — the important one:** is their avoidance driven by *self-interest*, not ignorance? If they simply haven't noticed, you have a lead, not a power; they will copy it. If copying would undermine their status, billing model, or identity, the barrier is durable.
- **Examples:** publishing openly where peers rely on scarcity; productizing where peers bill hourly; aggressively adopting new tooling where peers' identity is tied to manual craft; working across two fields where specialists in each lose standing by appearing unfocused.
- **Decay:** counter-positioning always erodes once the incumbent position stops being worth defending. Note the expected half-life.

### 4. Switching Costs → Replacement cost, with a warning

*Business version: customers face real cost to change vendors.*
**Personal version:** what it would cost your employer or clients to replace you.

- **Benefit test:** if you left, what would be expensive to rebuild — context, relationships, systems you designed, tacit institutional knowledge, trust with specific people?
- **Barrier test:** does replacement require *time* rather than money? Time-based barriers are real; skill-based ones are usually purchasable.

**The inversion that matters for people — this power can be a trap.** In business, switching costs are unambiguously good. For a person, high switching costs generate leverage **only if you would be willing to leave**. Switching costs built on non-portable, organization-specific knowledge make you indispensable in exactly one place, which is indistinguishable from being stuck. Always assess switching costs *alongside* portability (Step 3):

| | Portable value high | Portable value low |
|---|---|---|
| **Switching costs high** | **Real leverage** — they need you, you have options | **Golden handcuffs** — valuable in one place only |
| **Switching costs low** | Free agent — mobile but replaceable | Weakest position |

### 5. Branding → Pre-sold reputation

*Business version: buyers choose you habitually, without comparison.*
**Personal version:** decision-makers choose you without running a full search, because your name already carries an expected outcome.

- **Benefit test:** do opportunities arrive **pre-sold** — inbound, where the person has already decided before speaking to you? That is the observable signature. Being well-regarded once people meet you is not branding; it is competence that still requires a sales process.
- **Barrier test:** brand requires consistent signal over years and cannot be bought or rushed. But note *what* the brand is for — a brand precisely calibrated to a field you are leaving transfers poorly.
- **How to build:** repetition on one identifiable thing. Brand comes from being known for something specific, not from being visible generally.

### 6. Cornered Resource → Preferential access

*Business version: preferential access to a coveted asset on attractive terms.*
**Personal version:** access to something valuable that others cannot obtain at any reasonable price.

This is usually the strongest personal power when it exists, because it is the least imitable.

- **Benefit test:** do you control or have privileged access to something scarce — a dataset, an exclusive relationship, a rare clearance or credential, maintainership of a widely-used artifact, membership in a closed network, or an unrepeatable experience ("was in the room when X was built")?
- **Barrier test:** could a determined, well-funded competitor acquire it? If yes it is an advantage, not a cornered resource. **Genuinely cornered resources are usually accidents of timing** — being early to a field, present at a formative moment, or building the thing before anyone knew it mattered.
- **Critical caveat:** cornered resources depreciate if unmaintained or if the underlying thing stops being coveted. Audit both.

### 7. Process Power → Tacit craft

*Business version: embedded organizational routines producing superior output, hard to copy even when described.*
**Personal version:** a personal method that reliably produces better results and cannot be transferred by explanation.

- **Benefit test:** do you produce consistently better outcomes in some specific activity — problem selection, debugging, diagnosis, negotiation, writing, judgment under ambiguity?
- **Barrier test — the strict one:** if you wrote down exactly how you do it, could a competent person replicate it next month? If yes, it is a technique, not process power. **Real process power survives disclosure** because it requires accumulated pattern recognition.
- **The honest caution:** process power is the most over-claimed of the seven. People routinely mistake experience for tacit craft. The test is not "I've done this a long time"; it is "others who tried to copy this failed."
- **The commoditization frontier:** many methods that were tacit craft five years ago are now approximable by tooling. Ask specifically whether this method is still hard to reproduce *today*.

## Step 2: The strength-vs-power audit

The core deliverable. List everything the person considers a strength, and sort each one:

| Claimed strength | Benefit? | Barrier? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|

Verdicts:
- **Power** — both tests pass. Name which of the seven.
- **Commodity strength** — real benefit, no barrier. Necessary for entry, not for capture. Say so directly.
- **Latent power** — a barrier exists but the benefit is unrealized, usually through illegibility. Often the highest-return category, because the hard part is already done.
- **Eroding power** — barrier previously held and is now dissolving, commonly via tooling or field shift. Flag with an estimated timeline.

**Be direct about commodity strengths.** Most people's self-described strengths land here, and softening it destroys the analysis. The point is not that these are worthless — they are entry requirements — but that they cannot produce differential returns, and effort spent deepening them has low return compared to effort spent building a barrier.

**Apply the commoditization lens explicitly.** For each claimed strength, ask what a competent person with current AI tooling can now do. Skills that were scarce and are becoming abundant should be reclassified from power to commodity, on the record. This is uncomfortable and is the most valuable thing this analysis produces.

## Step 3: The person-specific overlays

Two dimensions that have no business-world equivalent and change the interpretation of every power above.

**Portability.** For each power, does it survive leaving your current organization? Switching costs and some network economies are often organization-bound. A power that exists only inside one employer is not a personal power; it is an asset that employer holds. Mark each: **portable / partly portable / non-portable**.

**Legibility.** Can decision-makers *perceive* the power without excavation? A power nobody can see produces no differential return. Mark each: **legible / requires explanation / invisible**.

These produce the second diagnostic grid:

| | Legible | Illegible |
|---|---|---|
| **Portable** | **Fully realized power** | **Latent — highest-return fix**, packaging only |
| **Non-portable** | Real, but exercisable in one place | Weakest — invisible and untransferable |

Powers that are real, portable, and illegible are the best available opportunity in any analysis: the hard part is done and only the communication is missing.

## Step 4: Power stack synthesis

Summarize:

1. **The stack** — the seven powers with ratings, ordered strongest to weakest.
2. **The anchor power** — the single strongest, which everything else should reinforce. Most durable careers have one anchor, not a balanced portfolio.
3. **Compounding check** — do the powers reinforce one another (cornered resource feeding brand feeding network) or sit isolated? Isolated powers stay flat; linked ones compound.
4. **Critical vulnerability** — the specific way this package fails. Name the mechanism: commoditization of the core benefit, decay of a cornered resource, erosion of counter-positioning as peers adapt, or non-portability if circumstances change.
5. **Arena fit** — does this stack match the arena from Step 0? A strong package aimed at the wrong market underperforms a modest one aimed correctly.

Then answer the question directly: **against the named foils, why would someone choose this person — and what would have to be true for that to stop working?**

---

## Step 5: Power-building plan

Powers are not equally available at every stage. Match recommendations to where the person actually stands (Helmer's power progression, adapted):

| Stage | Most available | Rarely available yet |
|---|---|---|
| **Early** | Counter-positioning, cornered resource (be early to something) | Brand, process power, network economies |
| **Mid** | Scale economies, switching costs, early brand | Mature process power |
| **Senior** | Brand, process power, network economies, leverage | Counter-positioning (usually spent) |
| **Transition** | Counter-positioning (again — crossing fields recreates it) | Switching costs (reset to zero) |

Recommend 3–4 moves, each naming: the power it builds, why it is available now, the barrier it creates, and rough time to maturity. Prefer **deepening the anchor power** over patching the weakest — Helmer's central practical claim is that power is won by intensifying an advantage, not by balancing a portfolio.

Distinguish clearly:
- **Realizing latent power** (weeks — packaging and legibility work)
- **Building new power** (quarters to years — genuine accumulation)
- **Defending eroding power** (urgent, and sometimes not worth doing — say when the right answer is to let it go)

## Guardrails

- **The Barrier test is the whole point.** If you find yourself rating something a power because it is impressive, stop — impressive is a benefit. Ask what stops a motivated competitor.
- **Never rate against an abstraction.** Always versus the named foils from Step 0. "Strong researcher" means nothing; "stronger than researchers who lack production experience, weaker than those with both plus a public artifact" means something.
- **Do not manufacture powers.** Some people genuinely have none yet — common early-career and after a field change. Saying so honestly, then showing which are buildable from here, is far more useful than inflating a commodity strength into a fake moat.
- **Do not confuse this with worth.** Power measures defensibility in a specific market, nothing else. Someone with a weak power package may be doing valuable work, and someone with a strong one may be doing harm. State this if the analysis lands hard.
- **Respect deliberate trades.** Someone may have chosen a path that forgoes power for autonomy, mission, or stability. Note what was traded; do not treat the choice as an error.
- **Commoditization is a fact, not a verdict on the person.** When reclassifying a skill as commodity, attribute it to the market shift, not to the individual's failure, and move directly to what is buildable.
